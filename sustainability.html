Case study · Sustainability and sustainable sourcing

Does certification make farms greener, and where is the biggest lever?

Companies sourcing food and agricultural raw materials have to show that their supply chains are efficient, low in emissions and responsibly managed. I looked at those questions from the farm up: what certification changes on 420 real farms, where emissions come from, and how to screen sourcing countries.

The work, step by step

Farm survey data, documented methods and open code, with the limits of each tool stated.

01

Test what certification really changes

My M.Sc. thesis: technical efficiency and eco-efficiency of 420 cabbage farms in three northern Vietnamese provinces (2022/23), using Data Envelopment Analysis and Tobit regression on survey data from CARES (Vietnam National University of Agriculture) and FiBL, building on Grovermann et al. (2024).

Organic-oriented PGS farms managed their inputs best (pure technical efficiency 0.79 vs 0.59 uncertified and 0.55 VietGAP), but their tiny plots (about 186 m² on average) made them the least scale-efficient. Being productive and being green were only weakly related (Spearman ρ = 0.32): a certificate or a high yield alone does not prove a farm is environmentally efficient.

02

Find the biggest lever on emissions

From the same farms: which inputs could be cut without losing harvest (DEA slacks), and how much each input contributes to the estimated greenhouse-gas footprint.

Fertilizer caused about 82–98% of the estimated emissions, pesticides about 2%. 52% of farms showed fertilizer slack and 26% pesticide slack. For a buyer, that points to a practical sourcing programme: better nitrogen management saves farmers money and cuts supplier emissions at the same time.

03

Account for farm emissions

AgroCarbon: a farm carbon calculator using IPCC Tier 1 methods for fertilizer, lime, diesel and electricity, with scenarios such as cutting the nitrogen rate or reducing tillage. For food companies, these farm emissions are part of their Scope 3 (purchased goods) under the GHG Protocol.

In both examples nitrogen dominated the footprint (77% for wheat, 89% for cabbage). Cutting the N rate by 20% saved 15–18%, reduced tillage only 1–4%. Every factor and its source is listed and tested. The example inputs are illustrative, not measured farm data.

04

Screen sourcing countries

FoodSecurity Atlas: a transparent risk index for 176 countries built in SQL from FAO and World Bank data (low or falling yields, volatile harvests, population growth).

A first screen of where supply could become fragile: Africa's median risk is almost twice Europe's (0.68 vs 0.36). It does not cover deforestation, labour or human-rights risks, which due-diligence rules such as the EUDR and LkSG require; those need supplier-level data.
